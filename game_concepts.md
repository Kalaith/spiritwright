# Spiritwright: Turning 10 minutes of fun into 10 hours

I think Spiritwright has a strong foundation, but it's approaching a common problem with autobattlers: the player can understand the game long before they've finished its content.

You already have quite a lot:

- 6 spirits with growth rates, personalities and awakenings.
- Modular golems with frames, arms, legs, sigils and materials.
- Elemental reactions between spirits and their bodies.
- A six-unit tactical formation.
- 30 campaign stages across five regions.

That's plenty for a vertical slice. In fact, you could add another 100 stages and 30 parts without necessarily solving the problem.

The missing piece is a reason to change a build that already works.

Consider the current gameplay loop:

Build golem

Win battle

Get reward

Get stronger

Repeat with approximately the same team

Once the player creates a decent team, much of the decision-making disappears. New content becomes something their existing team automatically defeats.

I would address this through three layers of progression.

| Layer      | Player's question                                                         | Timescale                 |
| ---------- | ------------------------------------------------------------------------- | ------------------------- |
| Tactical   | Can I beat this enemy with my current collection?                         | 2–10 minutes              |
| Collection | What new golems, spirits and combinations can I discover?                 | 1–10 hours                |
| Long-term  | What am I building toward, and what can I achieve that I couldn't before? | Multiple sessions or days |

Right now, Spiritwright has a good start on the first two layers. The third is relatively thin.

The important question is whether these three versions of Pyra are genuinely different in combat, rather than simply having different damage and defence numbers.

I would introduce build identities. For example:

- Shield + Kindling: an aggressive tank that burns enemies when attacked.
- Spirit Cannon + Crystal: a ranged elemental damage dealer.
- Aegis + Mending Rune: a protective support.
- Hammer + Spider Legs: a fast disruptive melee fighter.

Each build should have a noticeable strength, weakness and preferred enemy.

That makes experimentation useful instead of purely cosmetic.

## 2. Create encounters that challenge specific builds

I wouldn't increase difficulty by simply multiplying enemy health.

Instead, make certain opponents expose weaknesses in the player's otherwise successful strategy.

| Encounter       | Challenge                                   | Encouraged response                                    |
| --------------- | ------------------------------------------- | ------------------------------------------------------ |
| Ember Swarm     | Numerous weak attackers that spread Burn    | Ward, healing or area damage                           |
| Iron Sentinel   | Extremely resilient front-line defender     | Magic damage, armour-breaking or alternative targeting |
| Mirror Court    | Punishes repeated direct attacks            | Mixed damage types, different attack patterns          |
| Storm Choir     | Powerful back-row support                   | Ranged attacks, disruption or lane pressure            |
| Withering Grove | Long fights favour the enemy's regeneration | Burst damage or healing suppression                    |

The crucial distinction: these are puzzles with multiple valid answers, not mandatory equipment checks.

Players should think, "Maybe I could turn Odo into a damage dealer for this fight," rather than, "I need to grind another five levels."

I'd also let players inspect enemy formations and their key abilities before battle. The fun should come from preparing a solution and watching it work.

## 3. Make collecting spirits an ongoing activity

Six spirits are enough to introduce the game, but not enough to sustain a collection-focused game for days.

If Pyra, Neris and Odo are always the correct spirits to use because they're already levelled, rescuing additional spirits may feel like extra work.

I'd eventually aim for perhaps 20–30 distinct spirits, but without implementing all of them now.

More importantly, give players a reason to collect spirits beyond filling six positions.

For example:

Different growth identities. Two fire spirits could favour completely different playstyles. Pyra might scale with attack, while a second fire spirit might specialise in wards, delayed explosions or supporting allies.

Spirit-body compatibility. Keep your existing freedom to put any spirit in any body. Preferences should provide interesting bonuses or behavioural differences, never outright prohibit builds.

Expeditions. Spirits outside the active team could gather resources, investigate ruins or discover hidden construction recipes. This lets a collection become useful even when only six golems fight.

Spirit mastery. Using a spirit with different frames or materials could gradually unlock a small number of permanent, character-specific options. This rewards experimentation rather than endless XP grinding.

I would avoid randomised spirit stat rolls for now. Your existing growth-rate system already gives spirits identity, and adding randomness prematurely could make the collection harder to understand.

## 4. Add a workshop economy, but not a grinding treadmill

Currently you gain parts primarily through campaign progression. That's a finite reward structure. Once the campaign is finished, the reason to keep playing mostly disappears.

I'd add a small crafting and discovery system.

Imagine players recover schematics, materials and spirit fragments from different encounters.

The Golem Laboratory becomes more than somewhere you equip parts. It becomes a place to experiment.

Some possibilities:

- Dismantle unwanted parts to recover crafting materials.
- Unlock specialised variants of known parts by completing challenges.
- Discover recipes by combining materials with particular spirit elements.
- Construct additional vessels to maintain several builds without dismantling every golem.

One important design choice: let players freely swap parts on their active team.

Don't make basic experimentation expensive. Charge resources for discovering or creating new options, not for changing your mind.

## 5. Introduce a second game mode: The Shifting Crucible

This is probably the single biggest addition I'd make.

Your campaign can remain a handcrafted 30-stage adventure. But once the player understands the systems, offer a repeatable mode that demands new strategies.

THE SHIFTING CRUCIBLE

Concept

Modify a vessel

Unlock a relic

A Crucible expedition could involve 6–10 encounters and take 15–30 minutes. The team retains temporary bonuses throughout the expedition, creating interesting combinations that aren't available in normal campaign play.

Examples:

- All Stone bodies gain temporary regeneration.
- Fire spirits apply an additional Burn when protected by a Ward.
- Spider Legs attack twice after an ally is defeated.
- Each surviving golem grants the next one bonus magic damage.

These temporary effects would be layered onto the existing combat system, not require an entirely new one.

Why this works: You're creating hours of replayability from existing golems, parts, enemy encounters and combat mechanics.

However, I wouldn't make Spiritwright a full roguelike. The Crucible should supplement the persistent roster and campaign rather than replace them. Players should still care about the spirits and vessels they've built over time.

## 6. Give the player a collection of challenges, not merely a list of stages

Something I especially like for Spiritwright is a Golem Codex that records discoveries and accomplishments.

Consider the difference:

"Defeat the Root Warden."

Versus:

"Defeat the Root Warden with only Wood bodies."

Or:

"Defeat the Root Warden without dealing direct attack damage."

The second and third examples create a reason to return to an encounter with a completely different build.

You could group achievements into:

- Spirit trials: Win battles using unusual roles for specific spirits.
- Engineering trials: Win using particular frames, materials or part combinations.
- Tactical trials: Win under restrictions on lanes, units or status effects.
- Boss mastery: Beat bosses with optional challenges and modifiers.

Rewards could include cosmetic vessel finishes, rare schematics, new sigils and codex entries.

I'd make the first set available relatively early, rather than locking every challenge behind campaign completion.

This changes replaying a stage from XP farming into a new engineering problem.

## 7. Give players several simultaneous goals

A game that lasts days usually gives players several things they can reasonably work toward, so they're not just advancing through the next level.

Imagine a player opening Spiritwright and seeing:

The player can now decide what they feel like doing:

Progress the campaign, experiment with Pyra, collect something new or try a Crucible expedition.

Different goals can overlap, so even an unsuccessful boss attempt might advance spirit mastery or a construction challenge.

That is much more engaging than a single linear progress bar.

## What I'd actually build next

I wouldn't implement everything above immediately. I'd test whether each addition improves the game before expanding it.

| Priority | Feature                                     | Why                                        |
| -------- | ------------------------------------------- | ------------------------------------------ |
| 1        | Enemy previews and counter-build encounters | Makes assembling a team more meaningful    |
| 2        | 8–12 optional engineering challenges        | Reuses existing stages and bosses          |
| 3        | More build-defining interactions            | Makes changing parts genuinely interesting |
| 4        | Codex with discovery and mastery tracking   | Creates medium-term goals                  |
| 5        | Short, repeatable Crucible expeditions      | Creates replayable content                 |
| 6        | Workshop crafting and additional spirits    | Provides long-term progression             |

I'd also consider letting players construct more than six golems eventually, while retaining the six-unit deployment limit. Otherwise, building a new vessel always means replacing an existing one, which discourages experimentation and collecting.

## How much playtime could this create?

These would be design targets, not guarantees.

Current campaign

1–3 hours

Engineering challenges and mastery

+2–4 hours

Crucible and collection goals

+4–8 hours

Continued collecting and refining builds

Variable, potentially many sessions

Illustrative playtime goals, not measured estimates. Actual duration depends on battle speed, difficulty, build variety and player motivation.

I would avoid daily login rewards, real-time crafting timers or artificial XP walls. They're ways to stretch activity across days, but they don't necessarily make the hours enjoyable.

### One important design test

Before investing in significant new content, I'd create one difficult encounter that cannot reliably be defeated by the player's strongest existing team, but can be solved by changing their equipment, formation or tactics.

Not a level check. Not a gear check. An engineering puzzle.

Watch what happens:

- Does the player understand why they lost?
- Can they think of multiple possible solutions?
- Is changing a build quick and enjoyable?
- When the new strategy wins, does that feel rewarding?
- Do they want to experiment with another combination?

If these things work, Spiritwright has the foundation for much longer play.

If they don't, another 50 spirits and 200 stages will mostly conceal the underlying problem.

My biggest recommendation is to make Spiritwright a game about becoming a better golem engineer, rather than simply acquiring stronger golems. The autobattles are where you test your inventions. The workshop is where most of the meaningful decisions happen.

I'd be interested in taking one existing boss, such as the Root Warden, and designing three genuinely different ways to defeat it using only your current parts and spirits. That would provide a concrete test of whether your combat system has enough strategic depth to support hours of play.
